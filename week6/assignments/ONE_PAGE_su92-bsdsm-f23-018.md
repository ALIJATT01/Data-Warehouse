## What I rejected, and why no transform recovers it (24 rows)

Four classes, and each one fails on something a transform cannot supply. **Weight as text (9 rows):** values like `approx 7000`, `see note`, `TBC`, `--`, `unknown`, `n/a`, and `1.2e4` — an estimate is not a measurement, a note is not a number, and scientific notation is not the contract's plain decimal. Guessing a weight would move the totals, and the totals are the one thing cleaning must never move. **Dates that do not parse (6):** `00/00/0000`, `32/03/2026`, `31/02/2026`, `2026/13/04`, `Mar-2026` — a fact row without a real date has no DateKey, and without a DateKey it has no place in a time analysis. **Negative weights (5):** a shipment cannot weigh less than zero; there is no "negative shipment" the way there is a credit note, so these are sign or keying errors with no true value to restore. **Blank ShipmentID (4):** with no business key a row cannot be deduplicated, traced, or re-submitted — it is unidentifiable, not merely untidy. Every rejected row sits in `RejectLog.csv` with its file, line, raw key, reason, and full original row, so the source owner can fix it and put it through again. A reject is not a fix and not a deletion: it is a row parked with its evidence.

## What I routed to Unknown, and why that is not the same decision (21 rows)

Fourteen rows carry an origin no alias maps to, and seven carry a status outside the dimension — and all twenty-one loaded, with key −1, counted and published. The difference is what failed. A rejected row is missing its grain, its date, or its measure: it cannot be a fact row at all. A routed row *is* a shipment with valid measures; only a reference lookup failed. Routing keeps the totals true (those fourteen city rows carry 195,150 kg the company actually moved) while keeping the gap visible. Swap the decisions and both sides break: reject a routable row and the dashboard goes green while the quarter quietly loses real freight; route a rejectable row and you load a row with no date, key, or weight, and every time and total analysis built on it is suspect. The −1 counts are therefore not admissions of failure but the audit trail: **Unknown city −1 = 14 rows**, from six spellings — Keti Bandar Jetty (3), Kotri Junction (3), Wagah Border (2), Taftan Crossing (2), Torkham Gate (2), Chaman Border (2); **Unknown status −1 = 7 rows** — `held at customs` (4), `RTO` (2), `Hold` (1). Deleting the fourteen city rows would have produced a tidy 1,186-row fact table and hidden 195,150 kg; routing them is what makes the 1,200-row table honest.

## The index I would build, and what it costs on every load

**DateKey first, CustomerKey second.** DateKey has 31 distinct values across 1,200 rows (selectivity 0.0258, 38.7 rows per value) and every dashboard page filters on it; the fact also grows by date, so new rows append at the end instead of being inserted in the middle, which keeps range scans sequential. CustomerKey is the strongest second choice (40 values, 30 rows each — the best selectivity of any column a human actually filters on). Not ShipmentID: perfectly selective (1.0) but no dashboard query ever filters on one consignment, so that index would be pure write cost. Not ModeKey: filtered on every page, but three values at 400 rows each mean a lookup still reads a third of the table — the engine scans instead. The cost, as a number: **1,200 additional index-key writes per monthly load for the DateKey index** (one per fact row — write volume on the fact path doubles from 1,200 to 2,400 row-writes, roughly 19 KB of index storage); a second CustomerKey index adds another 1,200 writes. That is cheap against a dashboard that otherwise scans 1,200 rows times its joins on every page — though on a table this small, minutes-long dashboards are almost never an index problem in the first place: descriptive text carried in the fact instead of keys, a bidirectional relationship, or a measure recomputing a ratio per row will each defeat an index on their own.
